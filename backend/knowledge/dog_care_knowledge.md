# PAWFECT Dog-Care Knowledge Base

This starter knowledge base is general educational material for PawPal. It is not a clinical protocol and does not replace veterinary advice. Expand it only with reliable, reviewed veterinary sources and record source title, publisher, URL and review date for each imported document.

## Emergency signs

Seek emergency veterinary help immediately for difficulty breathing, collapse, unconsciousness, seizures, major or uncontrolled bleeding, choking, suspected poisoning, severe trauma, blue or very pale gums, inability to stand, or a rapidly enlarging or painful abdomen. Do not wait for symptoms to pass. Do not induce vomiting or give human medicines unless a veterinarian or animal poison service specifically instructs you. If a product may have been ingested, bring its packaging and estimate the amount and time. Keep the dog calm and call the clinic while travelling if possible.

## Eye care and eye warning signs

Eye pain can worsen quickly. Squinting, keeping an eye closed, cloudiness, a visible injury, sudden vision change, marked redness, swelling, or thick discharge warrants prompt veterinary assessment; sudden severe pain or injury should be treated as urgent. Do not use human eye drops, leftover prescriptions, or home remedies unless the veterinarian directs you. Avoid pressing on the eye. Note when the problem began and whether one or both eyes are affected. A photograph can help document change but cannot establish a diagnosis.

## Skin, itching and coat

Itching and skin changes can have many causes, including parasites, infection, allergy, irritation and other disease. Note the location, duration, spread, smell, discharge, hair loss, new foods or grooming products, and flea/tick prevention history. Arrange veterinary care when itching persists, spreads, disrupts sleep, causes sores, smells, oozes, or is accompanied by pain or lethargy. Facial swelling, breathing difficulty, collapse or rapid deterioration is an emergency. Do not apply human creams or give medication without veterinary advice. Gentle dog-specific grooming may help routine coat care but does not treat the underlying cause.

## Vomiting, diarrhoea and appetite changes

Record how often vomiting or diarrhoea occurs, stool appearance, water intake, appetite, energy, and possible access to rubbish, plants, medicines or unfamiliar food. Repeated vomiting, blood, severe lethargy, abdominal pain or swelling, dehydration concerns, or inability to keep water down needs urgent veterinary advice. Puppies, older dogs and dogs with existing illness may need earlier assessment. Do not give human anti-diarrhoeal or pain medicines unless a veterinarian instructs you.

## Food, hydration and nutrition

Use a complete and balanced dog food appropriate for the dog's life stage and individual needs. Keep fresh water available and make food transitions gradually unless a veterinarian advises otherwise. Portion needs vary with age, size, activity, body condition and health; use the food label as a starting point and ask a veterinarian to tailor the plan. Avoid known hazardous foods such as chocolate, grapes and raisins, onions and garlic, and products containing xylitol. If a dog may have eaten a hazardous food, contact a veterinarian or animal poison service promptly rather than waiting for symptoms.

## Exercise, play and enrichment

Choose activity based on age, breed traits, fitness, weather and health. Useful low-cost enrichment includes supervised sniff walks, scatter-feeding part of the normal daily ration, food-search games, short reward-based training sessions, and suitable chew or puzzle activities. Introduce new activity gradually and provide rest and water. Stop and seek veterinary advice for unusual breathing difficulty, weakness, collapse, pain or heat-related distress. Avoid strenuous exercise in hot weather.

## Anxiety, fear and behaviour

Provide a predictable routine and a quiet, safe retreat. Reward calm behaviour and allow the dog space; do not punish growling, hiding or fear. For noise sensitivity, reduce exposure where possible and offer familiar enrichment in a secure area. Persistent distress, sudden behaviour change, aggression, self-injury or inability to settle deserves a veterinary discussion; pain or illness can affect behaviour. A qualified reward-based behaviour professional may help alongside veterinary care.

## Puppy toilet training and learning

Keep training short, consistent and reward-based. Offer toilet breaks after waking, meals, drinking, play and naps, and reward immediately when the puppy toilets in the desired place. Supervise closely indoors and clean accidents without punishment. Provide safe social experiences at a pace the puppy can manage and follow a veterinarian's vaccination and parasite-prevention guidance. Sudden loss of previously learned behaviour or signs of illness should be discussed with a veterinarian.

## Grooming, ears and nails

Use dog-appropriate grooming products and introduce brushing, bathing, paw handling and nail care gradually with rewards. Avoid inserting cotton buds or tools into the ear canal and do not pour cleaners into ears unless directed by a veterinarian. Nail trimming should remove only a small amount at a time; stop if the dog is distressed or bleeding. Ear odour, redness, discharge, head shaking, pain, skin sores or painful paws should be checked by a veterinarian rather than treated with home remedies.

## Parasite prevention and routine wellness

Ask a veterinarian for a prevention plan appropriate to the dog's age, location, lifestyle and health. Keep records of vaccinations, parasite prevention, weight, appetite, stool changes and veterinary visits. Do not combine parasite products or change dosing schedules without professional advice. A routine wellness visit is a good time to discuss dental care, nutrition, exercise, behaviour and preventive screening.

## Responsible use of AI and image screening

A chatbot cannot examine a dog, confirm a diagnosis or replace a veterinarian. Symptoms overlap across many conditions and text descriptions or photographs may be misleading. PAWFECT's eye and skin image classifiers are experimental screening aids; their output is a model prediction, not a confirmed diagnosis, and a low-confidence or apparently normal result does not rule out disease. Use the result as a prompt for a veterinary conversation, not as a basis for treatment decisions.
